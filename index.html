Find your retirement number and a plan for reaching it

Retirement planning can be broken into two distinct but connected steps. First you define the destination. Then you map out the path to get there.

Step One: How much will you need for retirement?

Estimating how much you will need for retirement can be difficult, especially if retirement is decades away. You can start by asking yourself a few questions:

How much do you expect to spend in retirement?

A good place to start is to think about how much you would need each month to live comfortably. For example, if $5,000 a month ($60,000 a year) would cover your needs and let you enjoy some of the things you want, that’s your starting point.

Not sure? Start with what you spend now. Your estimate will get clearer as retirement gets closer.

Jasmine’s example

Jasmine is 27 and earns $70,000 a year. She’s starting to plan for retirement. As a starting point she uses her current lifestyle, which she hopes to maintain in retirement. She estimates she’ll spend $70,000 a year in retirement, in today’s dollars.

How long do you expect to be retired?

Your retirement length is the number of years between when you stop working and the age you expect to live to.

Many plan to retire at 65 and Americans who reach 65 can expect to live for about 20 more years on average, according to the CDC. Many live longer so planning for longer periods is reasonable.

If you think you may want to retire sooner, or expect to live longer, you will have to cover more years.

Jasmine’s example

Jasmine plans to retire at 65 for a 30-year retirement, to age 95.

How much do you expect your investments to grow in retirement?

Retirement savings are usually invested so that they can keep growing, even after you stop working. The rate you expect them to grow, your expected return, is an estimate and it depends on what you invest in.

In retirement, many people hold more relatively safe investments, such as bonds, which typically come with lower returns.

Have you considered inflation?

Inflation, the fact that prices rise over time, means that the same amount of money today will buy less in the future.

The Federal Reserve aims for inflation of 2% a year. At that rate, prices double every 35 years, so $5,000 a month in 35 years would buy what about $2,500 buys today.

In retirement planning, you don’t have to guess future prices. Instead you keep everything in today’s dollars and adjust your expected return for inflation.

Jasmine’s example

Jasmine expects her investments to earn an annual rate of 4%. To adjust for inflation, she uses the target inflation rate of 2%. She estimates a 2% return.

4% − 2% = 2%

Will you have other income, such as a pension or Social Security?

Other income sources in retirement can lower how much you need to save. For example, if you expect to spend $5,000 a month and you will receive a $2,500 pension, half of your spending is already covered.

Step Two: How much should you save each year to get there?

The amount you need to save each year depends on:

  1. How many years you have until retirement
    • The further away retirement is, the less you have to save each year due to compound growth (where you earn interest not just on what you contribute but also on the interest you already earned). Waiting to save for retirement means having to save a greater amount each year to reach the same goal.
  2. What your investments earn each year
    • What you invest in, such as stocks, bonds or cash, shapes how much your savings grow. So does how you diversify risk and what you pay in fees. Learn the fundamentals of investing here
  3. Whether your employer provides matching contributions
    • If you have an employer that matches part of your retirement contributions, that’s money added to your savings on top of your own, which can lower how much you need to save yourself each year.
