Find your retirement number and a plan for reaching it

Retirement planning can be broken into two distinct but connected steps. First you define the destination. Then you map out the path to get there.

Step One: Estimate how much you’ll need

Estimating how much you will need can be difficult, especially if retirement is decades away. Start with asking yourself a few questions:

How much do you expect to spend in retirement?

A good place to start is to think about how much you would need each month to live comfortably. For example, if $5,000 a month ($60,000 a year) would cover your needs and let you enjoy some of the things you want, that’s your starting point.

Not sure? Start with what you spend now. Your estimate will get clearer as retirement gets closer.

If you know you will have other sources of income in retirement, like a pension or Social Security, you can subtract the income you expect from your expected spending to find the amount your savings needs to cover.

How long do you expect to be retired?

Your retirement length is the number of years between when you stop working and the age you expect to live to.

Many people plan to retire at 65. Americans who reach 65 can expect to live about 20 more years on average, according to the CDC. Many live longer, so planning for longer periods, 25 or 30 years, is reasonable.

If you’d like to retire sooner, or expect to live longer, you’ll have to cover more years.

How much do you expect your investments to grow in retirement?

Your expected return is how much you expect your savings to grow each year. It is an estimate and it depends on what you invest in.

In retirement, many people hold more relatively safe investments, such as bonds, which typically come with lower returns. A 4% return, before inflation, is a common assumption for retirement savings held in lower-risk investments.

Have you considered inflation?

Inflation is the rise in prices over time. It means the same amount of money today will buy less in the future.

The Federal Reserve aims for inflation of 2% a year, although it has run higher in recent years. At 2%, prices double every 35 years, so $5,000 a month in 35 years would buy what about $2,500 buys today.

In retirement planning, you don’t have to guess future prices. Instead you keep everything in today’s dollars by subtracting inflation from your expected return.

Example: Putting it all together

Suppose you estimate you’ll need $60,000 a year in retirement to live comfortably. You want to retire a little early, at 60, and hope to live to 90, a 30-year retirement.

You plan on holding your savings in relatively safe investments that generate a return. You expect a return of 4% before inflation. Subtracting the target inflation rate of 2%, you plan on 2% growth.

Your retirement number: $1,343,787 With this saved in today’s dollars by age 60, you can spend what $60,000 buys today every year until 90, when your savings run out. If you had ignored inflation and used 4%, the number would be $1,037,522, about $300,000 less, and you could spend what $46,325 buys today.
The math behind the number:
PV=Cr(1−1(1+r)T)
  • PV = the retirement number (what we’re solving for)
  • C = 60,000 (yearly retirement spending)
  • r = 0.02 (a 2% return after inflation)
  • T = 30 (years of retirement)

With the numbers filled in:

PV=60,0000.02(1−1(1.02)30)
  1. 60,000 ÷ 0.02 = 3,000,000
  2. (1.02)30 = 1.811362, and 1 ÷ 1.811362 = 0.552071
  3. 1 − 0.552071 = 0.447929
  4. 3,000,000 × 0.447929 = $1,343,787

Step Two: How much should you save each year to get there?

The amount you need to save each year depends on:

  1. How many years you have until retirement
    • The further away retirement is, the less you have to save each year due to compound growth (where you earn interest not just on what you contribute but also on the interest you already earned). Waiting to save for retirement means having to save a greater amount each year to reach the same goal.
  2. What your investments earn each year
    • What you invest in, such as stocks, bonds or cash, shapes how much your savings grow. So does how you diversify risk and what you pay in fees. Learn the fundamentals of investing here
  3. Whether your employer provides matching contributions
    • If you have an employer that matches part of your retirement contributions, that’s money added to your savings on top of your own, which can lower how much you need to save yourself each year.
